    97→[]{#_Toc100000 .anchor}**1. Executive Summary**
    98→
    99→This report is the sixth foundational document in the AI builder
   100→playbook, and it addresses the question of how multi-agent AI builders
   101→collaborate without conflict, how they survive the failure of individual
   102→agents or of the entire agent team, and how the build continues without
   103→data loss when agents fail and when they come back online. The five
   104→existing documents specify the architecture, the prompting, the stack,
   105→the operational disciplines, and the scalability and concurrency
   106→patterns; this sixth document specifies the multi-agent collaboration,
   107→the fault tolerance, and the continuity patterns that ensure the build
   108→completes regardless of which agents fail and when. The document exists
   109→because multi-agent AI systems, as practitioner sources document, fail
   110→at rates exceeding 50 percent in production, and the failures are
   111→structural rather than model-specific, meaning that a stronger model
   112→does not fix them and that only the architectural patterns this document
   113→specifies can.
   114→
   115→The document is organized into five parts. Part I establishes the
   116→foundations, including the multi-agent failure data and the
   117→collaboration requirements. Part II covers the multi-agent collaboration
   118→architecture, including the orchestration patterns, the task assignment,
   119→the state sharing, and the inter-agent communication. Part III covers
   120→the fault tolerance and the durable execution, including the checkpoint
   121→and restart patterns, the durable execution runtimes such as Temporal,
   122→Inngest, and Restate, and the idempotent recovery that prevents the
   123→duplicate side effects. Part IV covers the continuity and the graceful
   124→degradation, including the solo builder takeover when the agent team
   125→fails, the agent health monitoring, and the resumption when the agents
   126→come back online. Part V covers the counterarguments, the conclusion,
   127→and the references.
   128→
   129→The central finding is that the continuity of a multi-agent build is not
   130→a property of the agents but a property of the orchestration layer that
   131→coordinates them, and the property is achieved through the durable
   132→execution pattern that persists the state at every step, that recovers
   133→at the failing step rather than at the nearest checkpoint, and that
   134→ensures the build resumes without data loss regardless of which agents
   135→fail. An AI builder system that is orchestrated by a durable execution
   136→runtime will complete the build even if every agent fails and restarts
   137→multiple times, because the runtime reconstructs the state and resumes
   138→from the exact point of failure. An AI builder system that is not
   139→orchestrated by a durable execution runtime will lose the
   140→work-in-progress when an agent fails, will restart from the beginning or
   141→from the nearest checkpoint, and will produce the duplicate side effects
   142→and the data inconsistencies that destroy the build\'s integrity.

   144→[]{#_Toc100001 .anchor}**2. Opening Hook and Thesis**
   145→
   146→The user\'s question, asked plainly, is how the multi-agent AI builders
   147→collaborate exactly and properly, how they understand each other and
   148→follow the whole process, how they handle the failure of individual
   149→agents or of the entire agent team, and how the solo or main builder
   150→takes over without data loss and resumes the process when the agents
   151→come back online. The question is not hypothetical; it is the question
   152→that every builder of a multi-agent system encounters, and it is the
   153→question that the practitioner literature has been documenting
   154→throughout 2025 and 2026. Multi-agent AI systems fail at high rates,
   155→often exceeding 50 percent across production workloads, and the failures
   156→are systemic, not model-specific, meaning that a stronger model does not
   157→correct them and that the correction requires the architectural patterns
   158→this document specifies.
   159→
   160→The failure modes are well documented. Two agents book the same
   161→resource, because neither knew the other had claimed it. A third agent
   162→overwrites the file the second was still using, because the file lock
   163→was not coordinated. A fourth agent loops forever waiting on a fifth
   164→that already gave up, because the timeout and the failure propagation
   165→were not designed. An agent calls an external API, the API returns a 503
   166→at step 4 of 7, and the entire workflow restarts from scratch, burning
   167→compute, duplicating the side effects, and leaving the build in an
   168→inconsistent state. These failures are not the agents being stupid; they
   169→are the orchestration layer being absent, and the orchestration layer is
   170→what this document specifies. The hard problem in agentic AI in 2026 is
   171→no longer making an agent smart enough; it is making a team of agents
   172→work together without stepping on each other, and that is an
   173→orchestration problem, solved with architecture, not with cleverness.
   174→
   175→The thesis is therefore the following. A multi-agent AI builder system
   176→collaborates without conflict, survives the failure of any subset of
   177→agents, and resumes without data loss only when it is orchestrated by a
   178→durable execution runtime that persists the state at every step, that
   179→recovers at the failing step, that coordinates the task assignment and
   180→the state sharing through a shared ledger, and that implements the
   181→graceful degradation that allows the solo builder to take over when the
   182→agent team is unavailable. The durable execution runtime is the
   183→component that converts the multi-agent system from a fragile prototype
   184→into a production-grade build system, and it is the component that this
   185→document directs the AI builder to implement.

   189→[]{#_Toc100003 .anchor}**3.1 Key Definitions**
   190→
   191→Before proceeding, it is necessary to define the key terms. A
   192→multi-agent AI builder system is a system in which multiple AI agents,
   193→each with a defined role, collaborate to construct a software system,
   194→with the collaboration coordinated by an orchestration layer. The
   195→orchestration layer is the component that decomposes the build into
   196→tasks, assigns the tasks to the agents, tracks the task state, and
   197→integrates the outputs. A durable execution runtime is a runtime that
   198→guarantees the completion of a workflow despite failures, by persisting
   199→the state at every step, by replaying the workflow from the persisted
   200→state after a crash, and by handling the retries and the compensations
   201→automatically. Durable execution is distinct from checkpointing: a
   202→checkpoint saves the state at a boundary and restarts from that
   203→boundary, while durable execution recovers at the failing step, with the
   204→intermediate side effects recorded and not re-executed.
   205→
   206→Graceful degradation is the property of a system to continue operating,
   207→possibly with reduced functionality, when a component fails, and it is
   208→the property that allows the solo builder to take over when the agent
   209→team is unavailable. The solo builder is the single agent, or the human
   210→builder, that handles the tasks when the multi-agent team cannot, and
   211→the takeover is the transition from the multi-agent mode to the solo
   212→mode that must be seamless and must not lose the work-in-progress. The
   213→agent health monitoring is the discipline of continuously checking the
   214→availability and the performance of the agents, of detecting the
   215→failures, and of triggering the failover. The resumption is the process
   216→of bringing the failed agents back online, of synchronizing their state
   217→with the current build state, and of reintegrating them into the agent
   218→team.

   261→[]{#_Toc100006 .anchor}**4. Multi-Agent Collaboration Architecture**
   262→
   263→The multi-agent collaboration architecture is the structural pattern by
   264→which the agents work together, and it is the pattern that most
   265→determines whether the collaboration succeeds or fails. The Azure
   266→Architecture Center\'s guide to AI agent design patterns documents that
   267→the agent architectures exist on a spectrum of complexity, and that each
   268→level introduces coordination overhead, latency, and cost, with the
   269→recommendation to use the lowest level of complexity that reliably meets
   270→the requirements. The architecture must be chosen deliberately, because
   271→the wrong choice produces the coordination gaps and the conflicts that
   272→the practitioner literature documents.
   273→
   274→[]{#_Toc100007 .anchor}**4.1 The Orchestration Patterns**
   275→
   276→Practitioner sources document five orchestration patterns that dominate
   277→the production multi-agent systems in 2026: the supervisor, the fan-out,
   278→the pipeline, the debate, and the swarm. The supervisor pattern has one
   279→agent, the supervisor, that receives the task, decomposes it into
   280→subtasks, delegates each subtask to a specialist worker, and assembles
   281→the results; it is the 2026 default for most builds because it provides
   282→the clear ownership and the single point of coordination. The fan-out
   283→pattern has a single task distributed to multiple agents that work in
   284→parallel and whose results are aggregated; it is suited to the tasks
   285→that can be partitioned without interaction. The pipeline pattern has
   286→the tasks flow through a sequence of agents, each performing a specific
   287→transformation; it is suited to the sequential workflows. The debate
   288→pattern has multiple agents argue different positions and a judge agent
   289→select the best; it costs approximately 2.5 times a single-model run and
   290→is suited to the high-stakes decisions. The swarm pattern has a large
   291→number of agents, up to 300 with the recent models, that self-organize;
   292→it is suited to the large-scale, emergent-behavior tasks. The
   293→recommendation for the AI builder system is the supervisor pattern,
   294→because it provides the clear task assignment, the single point of
   295→coordination, and the straightforward failure handling that the build
   296→requires.

   331→[]{#_Toc100008 .anchor}**4.2 Task Assignment and the Shared Task
   332→Ledger**
   333→
   334→The task assignment is the process by which the supervisor decomposes
   335→the build into tasks and assigns each task to a worker agent, and it is
   336→the process that must be coordinated through a shared task ledger to
   337→prevent the conflicts. The shared task ledger is a persistent,
   338→append-only record of every task, its assignee, its state, and its
   339→result, and it is the single source of truth that all the agents
   340→consult. When the supervisor assigns a task, it writes the assignment to
   341→the ledger; when the worker accepts the task, it updates the ledger;
   342→when the worker completes the task, it writes the result to the ledger.
   343→The ledger prevents the double-assignment, because a task that is
   344→already assigned is visible to all agents, and it prevents the
   345→lost-work, because the task state is persisted and is recoverable. The
   346→AI builder should be directed to implement the shared task ledger as a
   347→durable, versioned data store, with the PostgreSQL database or the
   348→durable execution runtime\'s state store as the backend, and to direct
   349→all the task assignments and the updates through the ledger.
   350→
   351→[]{#_Toc100009 .anchor}**4.3 State Sharing and the Inter-Agent
   352→Communication**
   353→
   354→The state sharing and the inter-agent communication are the mechanisms
   355→by which the agents exchange the information, and they are the
   356→mechanisms that must be designed to prevent the silent message passing
   357→that breaks under load. The state that the agents share includes the
   358→build context, the task results, the file changes, and the error
   359→reports, and the sharing must be through the shared ledger rather than
   360→through the direct agent-to-agent messages, because the direct messages
   361→are not persisted, are not visible to the supervisor, and are the source
   362→of the silent failures. The inter-agent communication should be through
   363→the published events, with each agent publishing its results to the
   364→event bus and the other agents subscribing to the events they need, and
   365→the event bus should be persistent, with the events stored and
   366→replayable. The Model Context Protocol and the Agent Communication
   367→Protocol, documented in the recent research, are the emerging standards
   368→for the inter-agent communication, and the AI builder should be directed
   369→to use them or to use the durable execution runtime\'s built-in
   370→communication mechanisms.
   371→
   372→[]{#_Toc100010 .anchor}**4.4 File-Level Coordination**
   373→
   374→The file-level coordination is the discipline that prevents the agents
   375→from overwriting each other\'s changes, and it is the discipline that
   376→the practitioner literature identifies as a common failure mode. When
   377→two agents modify the same file simultaneously, the second agent\'s
   378→write overwrites the first, and the first agent\'s work is lost. The
   379→coordination is achieved through the file locking, with the agent
   380→acquiring a lock on the file before modifying it and releasing the lock
   381→after, and through the version control, with the agent committing its
   382→changes to a branch and the supervisor merging the branches. The version
   383→control approach is the recommended pattern, because it preserves the
   384→history, allows the concurrent work on different branches, and provides
   385→the merge and the conflict resolution that the file locking does not.
   386→The AI builder should be directed to use the version control as the file
   387→coordination mechanism, with each agent working on a branch and the
   388→supervisor merging the branches, and the merge conflicts should be
   389→detected and resolved before the build proceeds.

   391→[]{#_Toc100011 .anchor}**5. Fault Tolerance: The Durable Execution
   392→Pattern**
   393→
   394→The fault tolerance is the property of the system to continue operating
   395→despite the failures of its components, and it is the property that the
   396→user\'s question about the agent failures and the data loss directly
   397→addresses. The fault tolerance for the multi-agent AI builder system is
   398→achieved through the durable execution pattern, which is the programming
   399→model that guarantees the code completion despite the failures, by
   400→persisting the state, by replaying the workflow, and by handling the
   401→retries and the compensations automatically. The durable execution
   402→pattern has crossed into the early majority in 2025, with the new
   403→offerings from AWS, Cloudflare, and Vercel, driven primarily by the AI
   404→agent infrastructure needs, and it is the pattern that the AI builder
   405→system must adopt to meet the real-world standard.
   406→
   407→[]{#_Toc100012 .anchor}**5.1 Why Checkpointing Is Not Enough**
   408→
   409→The checkpointing is the practice of periodically saving the agent\'s
   410→state, so that after a failure the agent can restart from the last save
   411→rather than from the beginning. The checkpointing is necessary but not
   412→sufficient, because a checkpoint can only take the agent back to a
   413→boundary, and it does not record how far the code got between the
   414→boundaries. The Restate blog, a practitioner authority on the durable
   415→execution, documents the limitation: if a tool performs three calls, an
   416→API call, an email send, and a database write, and the process dies
   417→after the second, restarting from the checkpoint re-runs all three,
   418→because nothing recorded that the first two happened. The checkpoint
   419→describes the state at a boundary, not the progress within the boundary,
   420→and the limitation is the source of the duplicate side effects that
   421→corrupt the build. The durable execution, by contrast, records every
   422→side effect as a durable event, and on the recovery, it replays the
   423→events and skips the side effects that have already been recorded, so
   424→that the recovery lands on the failing step, not on the nearest
   425→boundary.
   426→
   427→[]{#_Toc100013 .anchor}**5.2 The Durable Execution Runtimes**
   428→
   429→The durable execution runtimes are the systems that implement the
   430→durable execution pattern, and the dominant options in 2026 are
   431→Temporal, Inngest, Restate, and Dapr Workflows. Temporal is the most
   432→mature, with the event-sourcing approach that replays the workflow code
   433→against the event history to reconstruct the state after a worker crash;
   434→it requires the separation of the deterministic workflow logic from the
   435→nondeterministic activities, with the LLM calls, the API requests, and
   436→the file writes implemented as activities. Inngest is the
   437→developer-friendly option, with the steps as the atomic execution units,
   438→the automatic retries, and the state persistence between the steps, and
   439→it requires no workers, no cluster, and no queue to run, just the
   440→functions in the existing application. Restate is the journaled
   441→approach, with the durable execution that recovers at the failing step
   442→and that handles the parallel work correctly. Dapr Workflows is the
   443→runtime-provided option, with the automatic state persistence at every
   444→await point and the automatic failure recovery through the durable
   445→reminders. The recommendation for the AI builder system is Inngest for
   446→the ease of integration, with Temporal as the alternative for the
   447→systems that need the maximum maturity and the event-sourcing
   448→guarantees.

   477→[]{#_Toc100014 .anchor}**5.3 The Workflow and Activity Split**
   478→
   479→The durable execution requires the separation of the deterministic
   480→workflow logic from the nondeterministic activities, and the separation
   481→is the discipline that makes the replay possible. The workflow logic is
   482→the deterministic code that orchestrates the steps, that decides the
   483→next step based on the previous results, and that must produce the same
   484→output given the same input and the same event history. The activities
   485→are the nondeterministic operations, the LLM calls, the API requests,
   486→the file writes, the random values, and the wall-clock reads, that
   487→cannot be inside the workflow logic because they would produce different
   488→results on the replay. The activities are recorded as durable external
   489→results, and on the replay, the workflow reads the recorded results
   490→rather than re-executing the activities. The AI builder should be
   491→directed to implement the build as a workflow with the activities, with
   492→the LLM calls, the file writes, and the tool invocations as activities,
   493→and the orchestration logic as the workflow.
   494→
   495→[]{#_Toc100015 .anchor}**5.4 Idempotent Recovery: Preventing Duplicate
   496→Side Effects**
   497→
   498→The idempotent recovery is the property that the recovery from a failure
   499→does not produce the duplicate side effects, and it is the property that
   500→the durable execution runtime ensures through the idempotency of the
   501→activities. Each activity is identified by a unique key, derived from
   502→the workflow id and the step id, and the runtime records the activity\'s
   503→result with the key. On the replay, the runtime checks the key before
   504→executing the activity, and if the key exists, the runtime returns the
   505→recorded result rather than re-executing the activity. The idempotency
   506→ensures that the recovery lands on the failing step, that the steps
   507→before the failure are not re-executed, and that the side effects, the
   508→file writes, the API calls, the database updates, are not duplicated.
   509→The AI builder should be directed to implement the activities as
   510→idempotent, with the idempotency keys, and to use the durable execution
   511→runtime\'s built-in idempotency rather than implementing it from
   512→scratch.

   592→[]{#_Toc100016 .anchor}**5.5 The Circuit Breaker for Agent Calls**
   593→
   594→The circuit breaker, documented in the fifth document for the external
   595→service calls, applies equally to the agent calls, and it is the pattern
   596→that prevents the cascading failure when an agent becomes unavailable.
   597→The circuit breaker monitors the failure rate of the calls to the agent,
   598→and when the failure rate exceeds a threshold, the circuit opens, and
   599→subsequent calls to the agent fail fast rather than waiting for the
   600→timeout. The circuit remains open for a cooldown period, after which it
   601→enters the half-open state, where a limited number of calls test whether
   602→the agent has recovered. The circuit breaker for the agent calls is the
   603→pattern that allows the supervisor to detect the agent failure quickly
   604→and to trigger the failover, rather than waiting for the timeout on
   605→every call. The AI builder should be directed to implement the circuit
   606→breaker for every agent call, with the failure thresholds and the
   607→cooldown periods based on the agent\'s reliability.
   608→
   609→[]{#_Toc100017 .anchor}**6. Continuity and Graceful Degradation**
   610→
   611→The continuity is the property of the build to continue despite the
   612→failures, and it is the property that the user\'s question about the
   613→solo builder takeover and the agent resumption directly addresses. The
   614→continuity is achieved through the graceful degradation, the pattern
   615→that allows the system to continue operating, possibly with reduced
   616→functionality, when a component fails. The graceful degradation for the
   617→multi-agent AI builder system includes the solo builder takeover when
   618→the agent team is unavailable, the agent health monitoring that detects
   619→the failures, and the resumption that brings the failed agents back
   620→online. This section covers each of these patterns.
   621→
   622→[]{#_Toc100018 .anchor}**6.1 The Solo Builder Takeover**
   623→
   624→The solo builder takeover is the pattern that allows the build to
   625→continue when the entire agent team is unavailable, and it is the
   626→pattern that the user\'s question about the solo or main builder doing
   627→the task without data loss directly addresses. The takeover is the
   628→transition from the multi-agent mode to the solo mode, and the
   629→transition must be seamless, with the solo builder picking up the tasks
   630→that the agents were working on, and with the work-in-progress
   631→preserved. The takeover is enabled by the shared task ledger and the
   632→durable execution runtime, because the ledger holds the task state and
   633→the runtime holds the workflow state, and the solo builder reads the
   634→ledger and the runtime state to determine what to do next. The solo
   635→builder, whether a single agent or a human, executes the pending tasks
   636→one by one, updates the ledger, and continues the build. When the agent
   637→team comes back online, the resumption process synchronizes the agents
   638→with the current state and reintegrates them.
   639→
   640→The takeover is not a fallback that is invoked only in the emergency; it
   641→is a mode that the system must be designed to enter and to exit
   642→routinely, because the agent failures are the norm rather than the
   643→exception in the multi-agent systems. The design principle is that the
   644→solo builder must be able to execute any task that an agent can execute,
   645→with the difference being the speed and the parallelism, not the
   646→capability. The implication is that the tasks must be specified with
   647→sufficient detail that the solo builder can execute them, and the
   648→specification is the task definition in the shared ledger, with the
   649→input, the output, the acceptance criteria, and the verification gate.
   650→The AI builder should be directed to design the tasks for the solo
   651→executability, to implement the takeover mode, and to test the takeover
   652→routinely, not just in the emergency.
   653→
   654→[]{#_Toc100019 .anchor}**6.2 Agent Health Monitoring**
   655→
   656→The agent health monitoring is the discipline of continuously checking
   657→the availability and the performance of the agents, of detecting the
   658→failures, and of triggering the failover. The monitoring is implemented
   659→through the heartbeat, a periodic signal that each agent sends to the
   660→supervisor, and through the health check, a query that the supervisor
   661→sends to the agent to verify its responsiveness. The heartbeat includes
   662→the agent\'s current task, its progress, and its resource usage, and the
   663→supervisor uses the heartbeat to track the agent\'s state and to detect
   664→the failures. If an agent misses a heartbeat, the supervisor marks it as
   665→suspected, and after a configured number of missed heartbeats, the
   666→supervisor marks it as failed and triggers the failover. The AI builder
   667→should be directed to implement the heartbeat and the health check, to
   668→configure the failure detection thresholds, and to implement the
   669→failover that reassigns the failed agent\'s tasks to the other agents or
   670→to the solo builder.
   671→
   672→[]{#_Toc100020 .anchor}**6.3 The Resumption: Bringing Agents Back
   673→Online**
   674→
   675→The resumption is the process of bringing the failed agents back online,
   676→of synchronizing their state with the current build state, and of
   677→reintegrating them into the agent team, and it is the process that the
   678→user\'s question about the agents coming back online and continuing the
   679→process properly directly addresses. The resumption is the complement to
   680→the failover, and it is the process that must be designed to avoid the
   681→issues and the missing data that a careless resumption would produce.
   682→The resumption process begins when the agent comes back online and sends
   683→a heartbeat; the supervisor detects the heartbeat, marks the agent as
   684→recovered, and sends it the current build state. The agent synchronizes
   685→its local state with the shared task ledger, reads the tasks that are
   686→pending, and requests the assignment. The supervisor assigns the tasks,
   687→and the agent resumes the work. The AI builder should be directed to
   688→implement the resumption process, to verify the state synchronization
   689→before the agent resumes the work, and to monitor the resumed agent\'s
   690→performance to ensure it is operating correctly.
   691→
   692→[]{#_Toc100021 .anchor}**6.4 The Graceful Degradation Layers**
   693→
   694→The graceful degradation for the AI builder system is implemented in
   695→layers, with each layer providing a fallback for the layer above. The
   696→practitioner literature documents the layered resilience model that the
   697→field has converged on in 2025 to 2026, adapted specifically for the
   698→LLM-backed systems. The first layer is the retry, which handles the
   699→transient failures through the automatic retry with the exponential
   700→backoff. The second layer is the circuit breaker, which handles the
   701→sustained failures by failing fast and by triggering the failover. The
   702→third layer is the fallback chain, which provides the alternative agents
   703→or the alternative models that can handle the task when the primary is
   704→unavailable. The fourth layer is the context compaction, which handles
   705→the context window overflow by summarizing the context and by retaining
   706→the essential information. The fifth layer is the bulkhead isolation,
   707→which isolates the failures to the affected component and prevents the
   708→propagation to the rest of the system. The sixth layer is the solo
   709→builder takeover, which handles the failure of the entire agent team.
   710→The AI builder should be directed to implement each layer, with the
   711→layers providing the defense in depth that ensures the build continues
   712→regardless of the failure mode.

   747→[]{#_Toc100022 .anchor}**6.5 The Complete Continuity Architecture**
   748→
   749→The complete continuity architecture combines the durable execution, the
   750→shared task ledger, the agent health monitoring, and the graceful
   751→degradation layers into a coherent system that ensures the build
   752→completes regardless of the failures. The build is orchestrated by the
   753→durable execution runtime, which persists the state at every step and
   754→recovers at the failing step. The tasks are assigned and tracked through
   755→the shared task ledger, which is the single source of truth. The agents
   756→are monitored through the heartbeats and the health checks, and the
   757→failures trigger the graceful degradation layers, from the retry through
   758→the solo builder takeover. The file changes are coordinated through the
   759→version control, with each agent on a branch and the supervisor merging.
   760→The inter-agent communication is through the published events on the
   761→persistent event bus. The result is a system that continues the build
   762→when an agent fails, that continues when multiple agents fail, that
   763→continues when the entire agent team fails, and that resumes correctly
   764→when the agents come back online, all without data loss and without the
   765→duplicate side effects. The AI builder should be directed to implement
   766→the complete continuity architecture, with each component specified,
   767→verified, and documented.

   871→[]{#_Toc100024 .anchor}**7. Counterarguments and Limitations**
   872→
   873→The recommendations in this report are subject to several
   874→counterarguments and limitations. The first counterargument is that the
   875→durable execution runtime, the shared task ledger, and the six layers of
   876→graceful degradation are excessive for a build that is not yet at the
   877→scale where the agent failures are frequent, and that a builder who
   878→implements them prematurely will incur the complexity without the
   879→benefit. This argument is valid for the simplest builds, where a single
   880→agent and a single workflow suffice. The counterargument fails for the
   881→multi-agent builds that the user\'s question specifies, because the
   882→multi-agent systems fail at rates exceeding 50 percent, and the failures
   883→are structural, not model-specific. The patterns this document specifies
   884→are the patterns that address the structural failures, and the patterns
   885→are easier to implement at the design stage than to retrofit after the
   886→failures have manifested. The recommendation is to implement the
   887→patterns for any multi-agent build, with the understanding that the
   888→patterns are designed to be implemented at the start and to scale with
   889→the system.
   890→
   891→The second counterargument is that the durable execution runtimes,
   892→Temporal, Inngest, and Restate, introduce a new infrastructure component
   893→and a new operational burden that a small team cannot sustain. This
   894→argument has merit, and the report acknowledges that the runtimes add a
   895→component to the infrastructure. The counterargument is mitigated by the
   896→managed offerings of the runtimes, with Inngest, Temporal Cloud, and
   897→Restate Cloud providing the runtime as a service, without the need to
   898→operate the infrastructure. The recommendation is to use the managed
   899→offering, which removes the operational burden and which provides the
   900→durable execution guarantees at a cost that is proportional to the
   901→usage. The cost of the managed runtime is far lower than the cost of the
   902→data loss and the rework that the absence of the runtime would produce.
   903→
   904→The third counterargument is that the solo builder takeover is a
   905→theoretical pattern that is not practical, because a single agent or a
   906→human cannot match the throughput of a multi-agent team, and the build
   907→will be delayed unacceptably. This argument is valid in the sense that
   908→the solo builder is slower than the team, but the argument misses the
   909→point. The takeover is not a replacement for the team; it is a
   910→continuation mode that prevents the data loss and the work-in-progress
   911→loss when the team is unavailable. The build that continues slowly is
   912→better than the build that is lost, and the takeover is the pattern that
   913→ensures the continuation. The recommendation is to implement the
   914→takeover, to test it routinely, and to use it when the team is
   915→unavailable, with the expectation that the team will be restored and the
   916→build will resume at the full speed.
   917→
   918→A limitation is that the document provides the patterns and the
   919→recommendations but does not provide the complete implementation, which
   920→is the work of the AI builder directed by the prompts. The patterns are
   921→the specification, and the implementation is the execution, and the two
   922→are distinct. The builder who needs the complete implementation should
   923→direct the AI builder with the prompts from this document and the prompt
   924→guide, and should verify the output against the verification gates. The
   925→document is the specification; the builder and the agent are the
   926→implementation.
